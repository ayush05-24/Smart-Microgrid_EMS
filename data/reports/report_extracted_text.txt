Executive Summary 
Traditional microgrids fail to economically manage the stochastic intermittency of solar 
generation and dynamic Time -of-Use (ToU) grid tariffs because standard rule -based 
heuristic controllers lack predictive foresight regarding lagging thermal HVAC loads.  
This thesis directly addresses this inefficiency by architecting a dual -layer Artificial 
Intelligence Energy Management System for decentralized microgrids.  
 
 
To accomplish this, a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) sequence-to-sequence neural 
network was engineered using a 5-year, 52,608-hour NASA meteorological dataset to 
accurately forecast 24 -hour localized array outputs. Subsequently, a Proximal Policy 
Optimization (PPO) Deep Reinforcement Learning agent was structured upon a 
continuous Markov Decision Process. This agent actively controls a simulated 180 kWh 
industrial Battery Energy Storage System (BESS). A SCADA -style dashboard was 
implemented to provide "Explainable AI" parameter transparency alongside IEEE -
standard hardware overrides.  
 
 
Findings indi cate the PPO algorithm successfully converged across 20,000 tensor 
timesteps. It autonomously executed extreme energy arbitrage pre-charging at ₹3/kWh 
and discharging to avoid ₹11.5/kWh peaks completely safely within the strict 20% –
95% battery limits. The architecture reliably reduced operational expenditures by 
12.23% against deterministic baselines, successfully insulating the microgrid operating 
budget even during an unpredictable +24% facility load surge.

CHAPTER 1 
INTRODUCTION 
1.1 Objective 
The primary objective of this research project is to architect, synthesize, and rigorously 
validate an intelligent, edge-compute capable Decision Support System (DSS) purpose 
built for decentralized microgrid environments. The modern electrical grid is 
undergoing a fundamental structural transformation: centralized fossil  fuel generation 
points are progressively being displaced by geographically distributed, localized energy 
resources (DERs) - solar photovoltaic arrays, wind turbines, and Battery Energy 
Storage Systems - that are controlled at the facility level rather than at the utility level. 
Successfully operating these microgrids at optimal economic efficiency demands 
millisecond-scale decision -making to exploit dynamic Time -of-Use (ToU) energy 
arbitrage: acquiring inexpensive grid energy during off-peak tariff windows, storing it 
within battery reserves, and deploying it precisely when macrogrid import tariffs 
escalate to peak rates. The central technical objective of this system is to 
mathematically mi nimize microgrid Operational Expenditure (OPEX) by replacing 
rigid, pre -programmed heuristic dispatch algorithms with an autonomous Deep 
Reinforcement Learning (DRL) agent trained via the Proximal Policy Optimization 
(PPO) algorithm, capable of predicting and acting upon shifting climatic and 
consumption variables in real time.  
 
A secondary - yet equally critical - objective of this project is to resolve the widely 
documented 'Black Box' transparency problem that has historically impeded the 
deployment of Artificial Intelligence in industrial IoT and power infrastructure sectors. 
Grid operators and facility managers remain hesitant to grant autonomous control 
authority to neural networks because the internal decision matrices of these models are 
opaque and unauditable. To address this, an explicit design objective was to engineer a 
professional-grade, industrial Human -Machine Interface (HMI) modeled visually and 
functionally after enterprise SCADA systems. This interface natively translates the 
continuously fluctuating mathematical action -space outputs of the DRL agent into 

2 
deterministic, human -readable Explainable AI (XAI) reasoning strings, provides 
physical SCADA style override controls, and enforces absolute hardware safety limits 
independent of the AI layer.  The project is time -bound to the academic year 2025 /26 
and is validated through extensive simulation using a five -year meteorological dataset 
and stress-tested against extreme load surge scenarios. 
1.2 Motivation 
The compelling global imperative to transition toward sustainable, renewable-dominant 
energy paradigms is fundamentally constrained by the severe stochastic intermittency 
inherent to solar photovoltaic and wind generation. Microgrids offer transformative 
potential for localized infrastructure - hospital campuses, heavy industrial 
manufacturing plants, university facilities, and smart city blocks - to operate with 
significant independence from the primary macrogrid, shielding themselves from 
rolling blackouts and exorbitant dynamic tariff surges. However, realizing this potential 
requires energy management intelligence that dramatically exceeds what conventional 
rule-based controllers can provide. Contemporary threshold -based energy controllers 
are fundamentally inadequate for this task. Hard -coded logical constructs of the form 
'if battery SoC falls below 40%, import from grid' lack any capacity for forward 
prediction or temporal reasoning. They are operationally blind to the future. Critically, 
they cannot proactively recognize that a sustained high ambient temperature at 14:00 
will inevitably produce a massive HVAC electrical demand surge two to three hours 
later at approximately 16:30 - a lagging thermal correlation confirmed empirically in 
the EDA phase of  this project. This temporal blindness causes reactive energy 
purchasing at the economically worst possible moments, nullifying the financial 
justification for owning expensive Battery Energy Storage Systems and rendering the 
investment in renewable genera tion infrastructure suboptimal.  
 
The motivation for this research is rooted in the practical gap between theoretically 
powerful machine learning models and their deployment in real physical energy 
systems and by the desire to bridge the wide chasm separating advanced academic Deep 
Learning theory from tangible, industrial -grade deployment. Prior academic work has 
established that Reinforcement Learning can outperform deterministic solvers in cost 
minimization tasks; however, industrial adoption has been slow owing to operator 

3 
distrust of autonomous AI decisions affecting critical infrastructure. By fusing a Long 
Short-Term Memory (LSTM) sequence -to-sequence network - capable of modelling 
the complex, lagged relationships between meteorological variables and facility load 
demand - with a PPO agent capable of acting upon that forecast through optimal battery 
dispatch, this project demonstrates that AI -driven EMS architectures can be both 
economically superior to legacy approaches and sufficiently transparent to earn 
operator trust. This project addresses both the technical performance challenge and the 
trust challenge simultaneously, by coupling a state -of-the-art PPO agent with a 
production-grade, SCADA -oriented dashboard that makes AI behaviour fully 
transparent and physically overridable. 
 
1.3  Background 
A microgrid is a locally bounded network of distributed energy generators, loads, and 
storage assets that can operate either connected to the central grid mode - synchronized 
with and drawing from the central macrogrid or in autonomous island mode  - 
functioning as an power island disconnected from the wider grid . In contrast to the 
traditional top-down utility model, microgrids incorporate bidirectional power flows, 
variable renewable inputs, and dynamic tariff structures, all of which demand 
intelligent, r eal-time control. Historically, EMS solutions for microgrids employed 
mathematical optimization techniques such as Mixed Integer Linear Programming 
(MILP), which operate on pre -specified objective functions subject to equality and 
inequality constraints. While provably optimal within their formulation window, MILP 
solvers are computationally expensive for real -time applications and assume a 
deterministic view of future states - an assumption that breaks down entirely in the 
presence of volatile renewable generation. 
 
Recent advances in Machine Learning - specifically Reinforcement Learning (RL), 
which is structurally formalized through the continuous Markov Decision Process 
(MDP) framework - offer a paradigm -shifting alternative. An RL agent enters a 
simulated environment without prior knowledge of its governing physics, interacts with 
it over thousands of training episodes, and autonomously deduces an optimal control 
policy through cumulativ e reward maximization. Once training reaches convergence, 

4 
executing the learned policy requires only a forward pass through the policy network - 
computationally completing in under one millisecond - granting the EMS the ability to 
issue instantaneous, near -optimal dispatch commands that respond to real time state 
changes without any computational lag. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), 
introduced by Schulman et al. (2017), represents the current sta te-of-the-art policy 
gradient algorithm for continuous action spaces of the type required for precise 
kilowatt-level battery dispatch control.

2.1  Review of Literature 
Microgrid energy management has attracted considerable research interest over the past 
decade, with a clear progression from deterministic mathematical programming toward 
data-driven and learning -based approaches. The following review surveys key 
contributions that directly inform the methodology adopted in this project. 
 
Kuznetsova et al. (2013)  were among the earliest researchers to demonstrate the 
applicability of Reinforcement Learning to microgrid energy management. Working 
with a grid incorporating solar generation and a single battery storage unit, they 
formulated the dispatch scheduling problem as a finite Markov Decision Process and 
applied standard tabular Q-learning. Their findings established that an RL agent c ould 
learn cost-reducing dispatch policies purely through trial -and-error interaction with a 
simulated e nvironment, without requiring an explicit mathematical model of the 
optimization problem. However, their tabular approach required discretizing the battery 
state-of-charge into a small number of coarse bins, introducing systematic 
suboptimality and scaling poorly to systems with high-dimensional or continuous state 
spaces - a limitation directly addressed in the present work through the adoption of deep 
neural network function approximators. 
 
 
Francois-Lavet et al. (2016) extended this work by introducing deep neural networks 
as function approximators within the RL framework, producing what is now referred to 
as Deep Reinforcement Learning. Applied to a microgrid scheduling problem, their 
Deep Q -Network (DQN) agent demonst rated significantly improved performance 
compared to heuristic benchmarks, particularly in environments characterized by 
complex, non-linear relationships between state variables. Their work established that 
deep neural networks  could successfully approximate the Q -function over large, 
complex state spaces relevant to real energy management problems, and confirmed the 

6 
importance of experience replay buffers and target network stabilization in achieving 
convergence. These foundational techniques are embedded in the Stable Baselines3 
PPO implementation used in the present project. 
 
Kofinas, Dounis and Vouros (2018)  investigated a multi -agent decentralized 
architecture in which individual Fuzzy Q -learning agents were assigned to separate 
microgrids within a networked topology, sharing aggregate demand information to 
coordinate energy flows across the network. Their us e of fuzzy membership functions 
to represent continuous battery states improved upon coarse tabular discretization, 
though the fuzzy Q -learning approach still required manual specification of the 
membership function boundaries and could not learn arbitrary non-linear policies from 
raw data. The multi-agent concept introduced in their work directly informs the future 
scope of the present project, which envisions extension to Multi-Agent Reinforcement 
Learning (MARL) across smart city microgrid clusters. 
 
Mnih et al. (2015) established the foundational methodology for human -level control 
through deep reinforcement learning using the DQN algorithm, demonstrating super 
human performance across 49 Atari game environments from raw pixel inputs. While 
not a power systems paper, t his seminal work introduced experience replay, 
convolutional feature extraction, and target network weight freezing as standard 
practice in DRL - all of which are incorporated in the PPO implementation used in this 
project. The work's dem onstration that a single algorithm with fixed hyperparameters 
could achieve expert -level performance across diverse environments provided the 
theoretical basis for applying DRL to the microgrid dispatch domain. 
 
Venayagamoorthy et al. (2016) proposed a dynamic energy management system for 
smart microgrids employing echo state networks for real -time load and generation 
forecasting, coupled with a neural network controller for dispatch optimization. A key 
contribution of this work was the explic it modelling of battery degradation as a time 
varying cost within the control objective, recognizing that repeated deep charge 
discharge cycles accelerate lithium -ion cell capacity fade. While the present project 

7 
does not model cycle -level degradation, this consideration - enforcing hard SoC 
boundaries of 20% –95% to prevent the most damaging operating modes - is directly 
incorporated into the reward penalty mechanism and edge override layer. 
 
Rocchetta et al. (2019)  applied a reinforcement learning framework to the joint 
problem of optimal grid operation and asset maintenance scheduling. Their treatment 
of safety constraints within the RL reward signal - specifically the use of large 
numerical penalties for policy actions that violated physical system limits - directly 
informs the constraint -enforcement mechanism in the present work's custom Gym 
environment, where a penalty of -10,000 is levied for any action that would breach the 
battery's electrochemical safety boundaries. 
 
 
Ji et al. (2019) conducted a real-time energy management study for a microgrid using 
deep reinforcement learning, specifically comparing DQN and Double DQN variants 
across multiple renewable penetration levels. Their results confirmed that DRL agents 
adapted effectively to varying renewable availability conditions and outperformed 
Model Predictive Control (MPC) benchmarks under high meteorological uncertainty. 
Importantly, Ji et al. reported that the inference sp eed of a trained DRL policy — sub 
millisecond per decision — offered a decisive practical advantage over MPC's iterative 
solving procedure, a finding that validates the algorithmic selection made in this 
project. 
 
Singh et al. (2024)  demonstrated machine learning -based energy management and 
power forecasting in grid -connected microgrids with multiple distributed energy 
sources, achieving an MSE of 2.002 for solar PV and 3.059 for wind power using 
Support Vector Regression. Their study  confirmed that ML -based forecasting 
significantly outperforms traditional linear regression across all accuracy metrics, and 
that combining accurate forecasting with intelligent storage dispatch produces 
measurable reductions in peak grid import. The SVR results provide a useful external 
benchmark against which the LSTM MAE values achieved in the present project 
(2.0748 kW solar, 3.5385 kW load) can be contextualized. 

8 
Spiliopoulos et al. (2025)  presented a Smart Microgrid Platform integrating AI and 
Deep Reinforcement Learning for sustainable energy management, reporting a 23% 
reduction in energy costs, a 40% decrease in grid dependency, and 85% renewable 
energy utilization. Their study employed PPO agents and found PPO to be up to 9.2% 
more effective in reducing annual costs compared to Rule -Based Controllers, with 
PPO's clipped surrogate objective identified as the primary factor in its superior training 
stability - a finding that directly corroborates the algorithm selection rationale in the 
present project. 
 
Dusabimana and Yoon (2020)  provided a comprehensive taxonomic survey of 
microgrid EMS methodologies, categorizing approaches into rule -based, optimization 
based, and AI -based families and systematically comparing their performance 
characteristics. Their analysis explicitly identifie s Deep RL as the most promising 
direction for future EMS development, particularly for environments where the 
operational state space is large, the reward signal is temporally delayed, and real -time 
responsiveness is non -negotiable - precisely the conditions characterizing the ToU 
arbitrage problem addressed in this thesis. The survey's identification of operator 
transparency as a critical barrier to industrial AI adoption directly motivated the XAI 
integrated dashboard design that constitutes a central contribution of this project. 
 
2.2  Project Description 
This project constitutes an exhaustive, end -to-end, full -stack intelligent Energy 
Management System for a simulated high -demand industrial microgrid facility. The 
architecture is explicitly structured into two decoupled processing environments: a 
Heavy-Compute AI Training and Inference Backend, and a Lightweight Edge-Compute 
Presentation Frontend - a separation mandated by the computational mismatch between 
the deep learning training phase and the real-time operational environment. 
 
The predictive intelligence layer employs a Long Short -Term Memory (LSTM) 
sequence-to-sequence neural network trained on 52,608 hours of longitudinal 
meteorological observations sourced via the NASA POWER Application Programming 

9 
Interface. The dataset spans five complete calendar years of hourly readings, capturing 
the full spectrum of seasonal, diurnal, and weather -event-driven variability in solar 
irradiance, ambient temperature, relative humidity, and wind speed. Two independent 
LSTM models were constructed: one forecasting solar generation output in kilowatts, 
and one forecasting facility electrical load demand. Both models incorporate advanced 
temporal feature engineering - including sine-cosine harmonic encoding of the hour of-
day to eliminate the discontinuity at midnight transitions - and rolling statistical features 
derived from three -hour and twenty -four-hour lag windows. The normalized 48 -step 
input sequences allow the LSTM's gated recurrent cells to capture the multi hour 
temporal dependencies that characterize lagging HVAC load re sponses to ambient 
temperature fluctuations. 
 
The prescriptive intelligence layer is a Deep Reinforcement Learning agent based on 
the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm, trained within a custom OpenAI 
Gym Python environment designated MicrogridPPOEnv. This environment 
mathematically enforces the electrochemical physics of an industrial 180 kWh Lithium-
Ion Battery Energy Storage System, including capacity limits, maximum charge and 
discharge power rates, round-trip efficiency losses, and hard SoC boundaries. At each 
hourly timestep, the environment presents the agent with a five-dimensional state vector 
comprising the current solar output, wind generation, facility demand, battery SoC, and 
applicable grid tariff. The agent responds with a continuous action scalar mapping the 
desired battery power flow and receives a scalar reward encoding the economic cost of 
the resulting grid import. Over 20,000 training timesteps, the PPO agent converged on 
a policy that proactively exploits the ToU tariff structure, pre -charging during the 
overnight off-peak window (₹3/kWh) and discharging during the evening peak period 
(₹9–11.5/kWh) to minimize cumulative grid expenditure. 
 
The presentation layer is a high -performance, fully asynchronous React.js 18 web 
application that functions as a simulated edge-compute node connected to the microgrid 
switchgear. Operating on a deterministic 1.5 -second physics tick driven by React's 
useEffect interval hook, the dashboard provides operators with live power flow 
telemetry, 12h/24h/48h predictive horizon charts rendered via Recharts SVG, 
timestamped anomaly alert logs, an Explainable AI banner that converts the agent's  

10 
continuous action scalar into readable English dispatch reasoning, and a SCADA-style 
Operator Override Panel implementing physical Island Mode, Force Charge, and Force 
Discharge controls that enforce hardware SoC boundaries independently of the AI 
inference layer. To maintain dashboard operability during backend disconnection 
events, a native JavaScript fallback simulator utilizing smooth trigonometric noise 
functions replicates the physics engine locally in the browser. 
 
2.2.1  Software 
The entire computational stack is built on open -source, freely available frameworks, 
eliminating direct software licensing costs for the project. The AI intelligence backbone 
is implemented in Python 3.10, with the LSTM forecasting models constructed using  
PyTorch 2.12 - chosen for its dynamic computational graph, efficient CUDA 
integration, and Pythonic tensor manipulation API. The PPO reinforcement learning 
agent is implemented using the Stable Baselines3 library (version 2.0+), which provides 
a standardi zed, research -validated PPO implementation compatible with custom 
OpenAI Gym environments. Data engineering - encompassing API ingestion, 
normalization, feature engineering, and DataFrame manipulation of the 52,608 -row 
NASA dataset - is performed using Pandas and NumPy. The backend inference and 
API layer is structured as a RESTful FastAPI endpoint, serving JSON payload 
responses to the frontend polling interval. 
 
 The frontend HMI application is built with React.js 18 and compiled via the Vite build 
tool for optimized bundle performance. Chart rendering employs the Recharts library 
for efficient SVG -based, animated time -series visualization, while the Lucide -React 
library provides scalable vector iconography throughout the interface. All visual styling 
is implemented through bespoke Vanilla CSS3 - eschewing framework overhead in 
favor of precise, hardware -accelerated backdrop-filter rendering for the glassmorphic 
panel aesthetics. Version control across the full project lifecycle is managed through 
Git and the GitHub platform.

2.2.2  Hardware 
The LSTM and PPO model training phases were conducted on a consumer -grade x64 
workstation configured with a CUDA -enabled NVIDIA RTX series GPU, which 
provided the parallel tensor computation acceleration necessary for efficient stochastic 
gradient descent over the large training dataset and the multi-episode RL training loop. 
The host system maintained a minimum of 16 GB of RAM to support in -memory 
manipulation of the full 52,608-row Pandas DataFrame without disk I/O bottlenecking 
during the feature engineering phase. 
 
The Battery Energy Storage System is implemented entirely as a software model. The 
simulated BESS specifications - 180 kWh nominal capacity, 55 kW maximum charge 
intake, 58 kW maximum discharge output, and a round-trip electrochemical efficiency 
coefficient of 0.965 - are encoded as hard physical constraints within the custom Gym 
environment, ensuring that all AI training and real -time inference operates within 
bounds that accurately reflect industrial Lithium-Ion hardware characteristics. The solar 
photovoltaic array is modeled at 55 kW peak generating capacity, scaled dynamically 
against the true local Global Horizontal Irradiance (GHI) and Direct Normal Irradiance 
(DNI) coordinates retrieved from the NASA POWER dataset. No dedicated hardware 
prototype was  constructed; the system is validated exclusively through software 
simulation. 
 
2.3  Goals 
The first project goal is to conduct a rigorous Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA) on the 
five-year, planetary -scale NASA meteorological dataset to identify, isolate, and 
formally characterize the complex non -linear correlations - including the 2.5 -hour 
lagging thermal indicator between ambient temperature spikes and HVAC load surges  
that render rule -based EMS controllers structurally inadequate for modern volatile 
microgrid environments. 
 
The second goal is to design, mathematically constrain, train, and deploy a Continuous 
Action Space Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) DRL agent that demonstrably 

12 
outperforms all standard heuristic threshold models in the exploitation of Time-of-Use 
tariff arbitrage, validated by quantitative comparison of grid import costs over identical 
test periods. 
 
The third goal is to engineer a fully production -ready 'Control Room' edge dashboard 
capable of rendering high-frequency telemetry updates with a 1.5-second refresh cycle 
without browser memory leaks or frame drops, providing operators with a continuously 
accurate view of all critical system parameters across multiple forecast horizons. 
 
The fourth goal is to overcome the primary barrier to industrial AI adoption by 
implementing an Explainable AI (XAI) module that dynamically maps the DRL agent's 
raw continuous action scalars to explicit, human -verifiable dispatch reasoning strings 
displayed on the SCADA interface, thereby providing the transparency and auditability 
required for operator trust in safety-critical environments.

4.1  Design Approach and Mathematical Modelling 
The complete system was developed through a structured, six-phase methodology in 
which each phase built upon verified deliverables from the preceding phase. The 
design approach begins with planetary-scale data engineering, progresses through the 
construction of a physics-bound simulation environment, continues through AI model 
training and convergence validation, proceeds through interface architecture and 
asynchronous integration, and concludes with robustness validation under adversarial 
stress conditions. 
 
4.1.1  Phase 1: Data Engineering and Exploratory Data Analysis 
The dataset was sourced via the NASA POWER Application Programming Interface, 
queried against the GPS coordinates of a representative mid -latitude industrial facility 
to obtain 52,608 hours of hourly meteorological measurements spanning January 2020 
to December 202 5. Key variables extracted included Global Horizontal Irradiance 
(GHI, W/m²), Direct Normal Irradiance (DNI, W/m²), ambient dry -bulb temperature 
(T2M, °C), relative humidity at 2 meters (RH2M, %), and wind speed at 10m elevation 
(WS10M, m/s). Facility load demand was derived from the temperature and irradiance 
inputs through a calibrated physical model. 
All input variables were normalized to the [ -1.0, 1.0] range using scikit -learn 
MinMaxScaler to prevent gradient dominance during neural network training - a critical 
preprocessing step given the orders -of-magnitude difference between raw GHI values 
(0 to 1000 W/m 2) and load demand values (38 to 120 kW). Temporal features were 
encoded as continuous cyclic variables using sine -cosine harmonic transformations: 
hour_sin = sin(2π x hour / 24) and hour_cos = cos(2π x hour / 24). This representation 
ensures the neural network understands that 23:59 and 00:01 are temporally proximate 
- a critical property for models learning diurnal charging patterns - and eliminates the 
spurious discontinuity at midnight. Rolling statistical features were computed over 

17 
three-hour and twenty -four-hour sliding windows: the three -hour rolling mean and 
standard deviation capture short-term generation trend momentum, crucial for detecting 
the onset of cloud -cover events, while the twenty -four-hour rolling statistics provide 
awareness of the current day's overall solar resource quality relative to the historical 
baseline. The final input feature vector thus contains: [ GHI, DNI, T2M, RH2M, 
WS10M, hour_sin, hour_cos, GHI_3h_mean, GHI_3h_std, Load_24h_ mean] - a ten-
dimensional ob servation at each hourly timestep, assembled into sequences of 48 
consecutive hours before being passed to the LSTM input gate. 
Exploratory Data Analysis confirmed three critical findings that shaped the system 
architecture. First, solar irradiance exhibits extreme bimodal volatility: output 
frequently collapses from peak generating capacity to near -zero within a single hour 
due to  localized adiabatic cloud formation, rendering smooth parabolic generation 
assumptions entirely invalid. Second, a statistically significant lagging correlation 
exists between sustained ambient temperature spikes and HVAC electrical load surges, 
with a measured time lag of approximately 2.5 hours between the thermal stimulus and 
the demand response - a relationship that linear regressors cannot capture but LSTM 
models can naturally represent through their internal hidden state. Third, the facility 
demand profile exhibits a severe evening peak coinciding exactly with the cessation of 
solar generation: base overnight load of approximately 38 kW rises to a morning plateau 
of approximately 60 kW, then surges to in excess of 120 kW between 18:00 and 21:00 
- precisely the window when solar output reaches absolute zero at sunset. These three 
findings collectively establish the empirical necessity of the dual -layer LSTM -DRL 
architecture adopted in this project. 
4.1.2  Phase 2: MDP Formulation and Physics-Bound Environment 
The microgrid energy dispatch problem was formally modelled as a continuous -state 
Markov Decision Process defined by the five-tuple (S, A, P, R, γ), where S denotes the 
state space, A the action space, P the state transition probability function, R the reward 
17 function, and γ the temporal discount factor. The state vector presented to the agent 
at each hourly timestep is defined as: 
          S_t  =  [ P_solar,t ,  P_wind,t ,  P_load,t ,  SoC_t ,  Tariff_t ]          …(4.1) 

18 
where P_solar,t is the current solar photovoltaic output in kW, P_wind,t is the wind 
generation in kW, P_load,t is the facility electrical demand in kW, SoC_t is the battery 
state-of-charge expressed as a percentage, and Tariff_t is the applicable grid import cost 
in ₹/kWh at timestep t. 
 
Battery dynamics are governed by electrochemical charge -balance equations that 
explicitly account for heat-loss inefficiencies. During a charging operation (net battery 
power P_bat < 0, i.e., power flowing into the battery):  
          SoC_(t+1)  =  SoC_t  +  ( P_bat × Δt / C_max ) × η_charge × 100          (4.2) 
During a discharging operation (net battery power P_bat > 0, i.e., power flowing from 
battery to load): 
     SoC_(t+1)  =  SoC_t  −  ( P_bat × Δt ) / ( C_max × η_discharge ) × 100       (4.3) 
where C_max is the nominal battery capacity (180 kWh), Δt is the timestep duration (1 
hour), η_charge and η_discharge are the round-trip efficiency coefficients for charging 
and discharging respectively.  In both equations, C_max = 180 kWh is the nominal 
battery capacity, Δt = 1 hour is the timestep duration, and η_charge = η_discharge = 
0.965 is the electrochemical round trip efficiency coefficient modelling thermal energy 
loss during Lithium-Ion intercalation and de-intercalation reactions. The agent's action 
scalar is mapped to a physical power command bounded by the hardware inverter 
limits: maximum charge intake of 55 kW and maximum discharge output of 58 kW. 
 
The net power drawn from the macro -grid at each timestep is computed via the nodal 
energy balance equation: 
         P_grid,t  =  max ( P_load,t  −  P_solar,t  −  P_wind,t  −  P_bat,t ,  0 )        (4.4) 
This formulation ensures grid import is always non -negative - the simulation does not 
permit reverse energy export to the grid - and that the agent's battery dispatch action 
directly mediates between the local renewable surplus and the demand deficit.

4.1.3 Phase 3: Reward Function Design 
The reward signal received by the agent at each timestep encodes the economic 
objective of minimizing grid import expenditure while enforcing strict hardware 
constraints:           
R_t  =  − ( P_grid,t × Tariff_t )  −  P_penalty          (4.5) 
where P_penalty is a large negative scalar applied whenever the agent's selected action 
would result in a state -of-charge violation (SoC_t < 20% or SoC_t > 95%). The first 
term penalizes the agent proportionally to the cost of each kilowatt-hour imported from 
the macrogrid. The second term P_penalty constitutes a large discrete negative scalar 
of -10,000 that is applied whenever the agent's selected action would drive SoC_t below 
the 20% electrochemical safety floor or above the 95% thermal runaway ceiling.  This 
penalty magnitude is calibrated to be substantially larger than the maximum single -
timestep energy cost, ensuring that constraint violations are treated by the agent as 
catastrophically undesirable outcomes to be avoided under all market conditions - 
including scenarios where importing from the grid is economically preferable to 
enforcing the constraint. A discount factor of γ = 0.99 is applied to weight near -term 
costs appropriately while retaining sensitivity to multi-hour tariff windows.

4.3  Constraints, Alternatives and Tradeoffs 
4.3.1  Constraints 
The most critical design constraints are the electrochemical safety limits of the Lithium-
Ion BESS. A minimum SoC floor of 20% is enforced to prevent deep discharge 
degradation - a failure mode that irreversibly reduces cell capacity and can cause 
internal short circuits in Lithium -Ion chemistry. A maximum SoC ceiling of 95% 
prevents thermal runaway risk from overcharging, a potentially catastrophic failure 
mode in high -capacity i ndustrial battery installations. These bounds are enforced at 
three independen t architectural levels: the Gym environment reward penalty, the 
FastAPI inference layer output clipping, and the SCADA override panel's hardware 
validation logic - ensuring they cannot be violated regardless of which layer 
experiences a software fault. 
Grid separation constraints mandate that grid import is forced to exactly 0.0 kW when 
Island Mode is active, regardless of the load deficit. A further computational constraint 

24 
arises from the complexity of the trained neural models: LSTM and PPO inference 
cannot be executed continuously on resource -constrained IoT edge devices, 
necessitating a lightweight JavaScript -based fallback simulation loop to maintain 
dashboard operability during backend disconnection events. 
A second category of constraint is the Edge compute limitation. The trained LSTM and 
PPO neural network models, comprising millions of floating -point parameters, cannot 
be executed continuously on resource-constrained industrial IoT microcontrollers of the 
type physically installed inside battery management switchboards. This architectural 
constraint dictates the separation of the heavy training phase (conducted on GPU -
equipped workstation hardware) from the lightweight edge inference phase, and 
necessitates the JavaScript-based fallback simulation engine that maintains dashboard 
operability during backend disconnection events. 
 
4.3.2  Alternatives 
Mixed Integer Linear Programming (MILP) was evaluated as the primary alternative 
optimization framework for battery dispatch scheduling. MILP offers provable global 
optimality over a known dispatch horizon and has a well-established history in energy 
management literature. However, MILP was rejected for three fundamental reasons: its 
computational complexity scales exponentially with horizon length and state 
dimensionality, making real -time recalculation infeasible during rapid generation 
transients; its re quirement for perfect forecast accuracy as an input assumption is 
violated continuously in meteorologically volatile environments; and its offline, batch-
solving paradigm cannot respond to sub -minute generation disruptions of the type 
produced by adiabatic  cloud formation events. PPO, by contrast, issues dispatch 
commands in under one millisecond once trained and inherently handles uncertainty 
through its probabilistic policy representation. 
Deep Q-Network (DQN) was investigated as an alternative RL algorithm within the 
reinforcement learning paradigm. DQN operates over a discrete action space, requiring 
the continuous battery power command to be quantized into a finite set of coarse power-
level bins. This discretization introduces systematic suboptimality in dispatch precision 
- the economic difference between charging at exactly 41.2 kW versus the nearest 5 kW 

25 
increment is non-trivial over a seven-day evaluation window. PPO, as an Actor -Critic 
method with a continuous Gaussian action distribution, avoids this quantization error 
entirely and was therefore selected as the primary RL algorithm for this project. 
Tailwind CSS was evaluated for frontend styling. While Tailwind's utility -class 
architecture accelerates component development through pre-compiled atomic classes, 
its constraint -based styling model imposes fundamental limitations on the 
implementation of non-standard visual effects — specifically the custom multi-layered 
backdrop-filter blurring, pulsing border keyframe animations, and dynamic opacity 
transitions required for the glassmorphic panel aesthetic. Bespoke Vanilla CSS3 was 
adopted instead, providing full control over hardware-accelerated rendering properties 
and enabling pixel -precise implementation of the industrial control room visual 
language. 
 
4.3.3 Design Trade-offs 
A fundamental trade-off governs the LSTM prediction horizon. Extending the forecast 
window from 24 hours to 48 hours nominally doubles the lookahead available to the 
DRL agent for ToU arbitrage planning. In practice, however, testing revealed that 
weather prediction uncertainty compounds exponentially with forecast depth: the solar 
output Mean Absolute Error within the 24-hour window remained at 2.0748 kW - well 
within acceptable bounds - but rose substantially beyond it as atmospheric chaotic 
divergence degraded the model's confidence. The system therefore anchors its primary 
dispatch logic to the 24-hour predictive array, where forecast quality is validated, while 
displaying 48-hour horizon charts on the dashboard for operator situational awareness 
- explicitly labelled as indicative rather than prescriptive. 
 
A second trade -off exists between training -phase computational intensity and 
inference-phase efficiency. PPO training over 20,000 timesteps against the full NASA 
dataset required substantial GPU -accelerated computation time. However, once the 
policy network weights reach convergence and are frozen as a static file, forward-pass 
inference requires negligible computational resources - well under one millisecond on 
standard hardware. This asymmetry between training cost and deployment cost is a 
defining property of deep learning systems and represents a highly favourable trade-off 

26 
for the production deployment scenario: significant one -time investment in training 
yields essentially zero marginal cost per dispatch decision thereafter. 
 
A third trade -off concerns the strict dark -mode glassmorphic visual design. By 
eliminating light -mode rendering capability and adopting the deep -slate industrial 
aesthetic, the interface sacrifices compliance with standard web accessibility guidelines 
(WCAG 2.1) for users with visual impairments. This decision reflects the system's 
specific deployment context - a professional industrial control room environment with 
trained operators working in controlled lighting conditions - and is explicitly 
acknowledged as a design limitation that would need to be addressed before 
deployment in any publicly accessible context.

6.1 System Architecture and Interface 
The complete EMS architecture comprises three hierarchical processing layers 
interconnected through well-defined interfaces. The Data and Training Backend layer 
orchestrates NASA API ingestion, LSTM model construction and training, and PPO 
Gym environment simulation. The Inference and API Middleware layer packages 
trained model weights into a FastAPI RESTful endpoint, serving real -time dispatch 
decisions and forecast arrays as structured JSON payloads. The Edge Presentation 
Frontend layer consumes these pay loads through React state hooks, rendering the 
SCADA Control Room dashboard at 1.5-second intervals. Fig. 6.1 illustrates this three-
tier architecture. 
Within the Backend layer, the training pipeline is segregated into two independently 
executable modules. The first module, lstm_trainer.py, handles dataset ingestion, 
MinMaxScaler normalization, sine -cosine hour encoding (hour_sin = sin(2π × hour / 
24), hour_cos = cos(2π × hour / 24)), three -hour and twenty-four-hour rolling feature 
generation, and the training of the two independent PyTorch LSTM models 
(solar_kw_lstm.pt and load_kw_lstm.pt). Each model is saved as a serialized .pt binary 
after training com pletion. The second module, ppo_trainer.py, instantiates the 
MicrogridPPOEnv Gym class, configures the Stable Baselines3 PPO model with the 
hyperparameters detailed in Table 4.2, and executes the 20,000-timestep training loop. 
The trained policy is seriali zed as a compressed .zip file (microgrid_ppo_policy.zip) 
compatible with Stable Baselines3's model loading infrastructure. 
The Middleware layer is implemented as a FastAPI application exposing two REST 
endpoints. The /infer endpoint accepts a five -element JSON array representing the 
current system state [P_solar, P_wind, P_load, SoC, Tariff] and returns the agent's 
continuous action scalar, the mapped XAI reasoning string, and the predicted grid 
import value. The /forecast endpoint returns a 24-element JSON array of hourly LSTM 
predictions for both solar generation and load demand for the upcoming twenty -four 
hours. Both endpoi nts apply input validation to ensure received state values remain 

33 
within physically plausible bounds before passing them to the model inference 
functions. 
The Frontend layer is structured around a single -page React.js application organized 
into five primary component zones. The Telemetry KPI Panel renders live numeric 
values for solar output, load demand, battery SoC, grid import, and current tariff - 
updated at each 1.5 -second tick. The Forecast Chart Panel renders Rechart SVG line 
graphs of the LSTM prediction arrays across switchable 12h, 24h, and 48h horizons. 
The Explainable AI Banner displays the XAI reasoning string returned by the /infer 
endpoint, col or-coded by action type (green for charging, amber for idle, red for 
discharging during emergency). The Anomaly Alert Log maintains a timestamped 
rolling list of detected system events - tariff threshold crossings, SoC boundary 
approaches, and manual override activations. The SCADA Override Panel provides 
three physical control buttons: Force Charge, Force Discharge, and Island Mode Toggle 
- each of which bypasses the AI inference loop and instead routes a deterministic 
command through a hardware -layer validation function that enforces SoC boundaries 
independently. 
A critical resilience feature of the frontend architecture is the JavaScript-based fallback 
simulator implemented in simulator.js. This module maintains full dashboard 
operability during backend disconnection events by locally replicating the microgrid 
physics engine using deterministic trigonometric wave functions. Solar output is 
approximated by a sine wave scaled to the current hour -of-day and attenuated by a 
smoothNoise() function that introduces controlled stochastic variance mimicking 
cloud-cover tran sients. Load demand is approximated by a compound sinusoidal 
function calibrated to the empirically observed three -phase daily profile: 38 kW 
overnight baseline, 60 kW morning plateau from 10:00, and 120 kW evening peak 
between 18:00 and 21:00. The fallbac k simulator runs at the same 1.5 -second interval 
as the live API polling loop, ensuring that the dashboard's visual behaviour is 
indistinguishable from live operation during brief connectivity interruptions.

7.2.1   EDA Findings and Environmental Characterization 
The Exploratory Data Analysis phase produced findings that conclusively validate 
every major architectural choice made in this project. The confirmed bimodal volatility 
of solar irradiance - with GHI output capable of collapsing from the 55 kW peak 
capacity to near -zero within a single hour due to localized adiabatic cloud formation 
events - establishes that any EMS relying on smooth parabolic generation assumptions 
will systematically mismanage battery dispatch during the most economically critical 
periods. A conventional rule -based controller operating on a smooth generation 
assumption schedules battery discharge during the afternoon hours expecting continued 


46 
solar availability; when a cloud transient severs generation mid -afternoon, the 
controller finds the battery depleted precisely when peak tariffs begin at 18:00, forcing 
costly grid imports at the worst possible economic moment. The LSTM forecaster 
directly addresses this failure mode by predicting generation collapses from the 
preceding atmospheric state indicators - elevated humidity, reduced DNI/GHI ratio, and 
sustained wind direction shifts - allowing the DRL agent to retain battery reserves 
during uncertain generation periods. 
 
The empirically confirmed 2.5 -hour lagging thermal correlation between ambient 
temperature and HVAC electrical demand represents the second critical EDA finding. 
The dataset analysis revealed that sustained dry -bulb temperatures above 
approximately 34°C at  14:00 consistently produce HVAC compressor load surges 
peaking at or above 120 kW by approximately 16:30, as the thermal inertia of the 
building envelope delays the interior temperature response by approximately 2.5 hours. 
This delay is precisely the type  of multi-step sequential dependency that LSTM gated 
recurrent networks are architecturally designed to capture through their cell state 
memory mechanisms - and precisely the type of dependency that static regression 
models, which lack temporal memory, cannot represent. The practical consequence for 
the EMS is significant: a system that can predict the 16:30 HVAC surge from the 14:00 
temperature reading can p re-position the battery at maximum charge by 15:30, fully 
absorbing the demand surge through battery discharge rather than expensive peak-rate 
grid import. A system without this temporal prediction capability cannot. 
 
The facility load profile analysis - which confirmed a three -phase daily demand 
structure of approximately 38 kW overnight baseline, approximately 60 kW morning 
operational plateau from 10:00, and in excess of 120 kW during the 18:00 –21:00 
evening commercial peak - established the economic severity of the control problem. 
The evening peak demand coincides exactly with the cessation of solar generation at 
sunset, the activation of the maximum peak tariff band (₹9.0–11.5/kWh), and the period 
of highest HVAC-driven load. This triple coincidence creates an exponential economic 
penalty for any EMS that enters the 18:00 window with a depleted battery: the full 
facility demand must be met from grid imports at peak tariff rates, generating the 
maximum possible hourly expenditure. The DRL agent learned to treat this window as 
the primary target of its arbitrage strategy - every training decision during the overnight 

47 
off-peak window was evaluated in terms of its contribution to battery SoC available at 
18:00. 
 
7.2.2   LSTM Forecaster Performance Analysis 
 
The LSTM validation results demonstrate strong predictive performance across both 
forecasting tasks. The solar generation forecaster - a two-layer LSTM with 96 hidden 
units per layer, 0.15 dropout rate, 48-step input sequences, and AdamW optimization - 
achieves a Mean Absolute Error of 2.0748 kW on the held-out test set. This corresponds 
to less than 3.8% of the 55 kW peak generating capacity, and demonstrates that the 
sine-cosine temporal encoding and rolling feature engineering significantly improve the 
model's ability to predict generation profiles relative to naive time -series approaches. 
The facility load forecaster, sharing identical architectural hyperparameters, achieves a 
Mean Absolute Error of 3.5385 kW on the validation set - less than 3.0% of the 
maximum observed peak demand. Critically, both models achieve these accuracy levels 
while operating purely on meteorological proxy variables rather than measured facility 
consumption data, confirming that the NASA POWER dataset provi des sufficient 
environmental signal to reconstruct the facility's operational load profile with high 
fidelity. 
 
The economic significance of these MAE values merits explicit quantification. At the 
peak tariff differential of ₹8.5/kWh between the off-peak rate (₹3.0/kWh) and the peak 
ceiling (₹11.5/kWh), a solar forecast error of 2.0748 kW translates to a worst -case 
economic mismatch of approximately ₹17.64 per hour if the error occurs precisely 
during the peak tariff window. Over the 168 -hour evaluation period, even if every 
forecast hour were in error by the full MAE - an extremely conservative assumption, 
since errors are distributed and partially self -cancelling - the cumulative forecasting 
error would be approximately ₹2,965. The actual total savings achieved by the DRL 
agent (₹5,035.25) comfortably exceeds this worst -case error estimate by a factor of 
approximately 1.7, confirming that the LSTM forecasting accura cy is more than 
sufficient to enable economically positive arbitrage decisions. The 24 -hour prediction 
horizon was confirmed as the operationally optimal window: tests extending the 
forecast horizon to 48 hours revealed MAE values rising to levels that would erode the 

48 
economic margin, validating the architectural decision to anchor the dispatch logic to 
the 24-hour forecast.

arbitrage policy. The reward curve plotted across all 20,000 training timesteps exhibits

exploration phase spanning approximately timesteps 0 –3,000, during which the agent 
samples the action space randomly and frequently triggers the -10,000 SoC constraint

convergence plateau spanning timesteps 10,000–20,000, during which the rolling mean

tariff constraints. The fact that convergence is reached at approximately the halfway 
point of the trai ning budget (timestep 10,000) provides additional confidence that the 
final policy is not the result of continued improvement but rather a stable, generalized 
solution.

development phases - from a rigorous five -year NASA meteorological data analysis 
through physics -bound simulation environment construction, dual LSTM and PPO 
model training, full-stack integration, SCADA interface development, and adversarial 
robustness validation - with all six project goals stated in Section 2.3 achieved and 
verified against quantitative success criteria. 
The key technical findings of this research are threefold. First, a two-layer LSTM neural 
network trained on publicly available NASA meteorological data can achieve solar 
generation forecasting at a Mean Absolute Error of 2.0748 kW and load demand 
forecasting at 3.5385 kW within a 24 -hour prediction horizon - sufficient accuracy to 
enable economically meaningful ToU energy arbitrage decisions. Second, Proximal 
Policy Optimization is an effective, practical, and computationally tractable algorithm 
for continuous-action microgrid dispatch, converging to a stable, constraint-respecting 
policy over 20,000 training timesteps and delivering a verified 12.23% reduction in 
operational expenditure against a rule -based baseline - equivalent to ₹5,035.25 in 
savings over a seven-day evaluation window. Third, the integration of Explainable AI 
transparency mechanisms within the operator interface is technically feasible using 
straightforward action-to-string mapping logic, and represe nts a practically necessary  
design elem ent for any AI -based EMS intended for industrial deployment where 
operator trust and auditability are non-negotiable requirements. 

54 
Several limitations of the current implementation are acknowledged. The BESS model 
enforces static SoC boundaries and fixed efficiency coefficients but does not model the 
gradual capacity fade caused by repeated charge-discharge cycling - a phenomenon that 
would meaningfully affect long-run economic projections over a multi-year operational 
horizon. The facility load model is derived from meteorological proxies rather than 
measured facility consumption data, which may introduce systematic biases in the 
demand forecast. The complete system has been validated through software simulation 
exclusively; hardware -in-the-loop testing with a physical BESS is required before 
production deployment. The interface's strict dark-mode accessibility limitation, while 
appropriate for the target deployment context, would require resolution before wider 
deployment. 
Three principal directions for future research are identified. The most immediately 
impactful extension is the incorporation of a battery cycle degradation model - for 
example, a capacity fade function parameterized by cycle depth, C -rate, and operating 
temperature - into the reward signal, allowing the PPO agent to learn a dispatch policy 
that balances short -term cost minimization against long -term hardware asset 
preservation. A second high -value extension involves scaling the system to a Multi -
Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) framework in which independent PPO agents 
governing separate microgrids within a smart city district negotiate peer-to-peer energy 
trading contracts across a mesh LAN network, potentially enabling entire urban blocks 
to achieve complete macrogrid independence during peak generation periods. A third 
direction is embedded hardware deployment: migrating the trained inference pipeline 
from Python on desktop hardware to a resource-constrained industrial edge controller, 
validating real-time performance characteristics and enabling the system to interface 
directly with physical switchgear and BESS hardware for live microgrid operation.
